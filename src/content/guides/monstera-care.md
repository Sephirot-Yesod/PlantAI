# Monstera Care: How to Grow Big, Healthy Split Leaves

Water a monstera thoroughly when the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of soil is dry, not on a fixed schedule [1,5]. Give it bright, indirect light within a meter (3 ft) of an east- or west-facing window, at 18 to 25°C (65 to 77°F) [3,5].

*Monstera deliciosa*, also called Swiss cheese plant or split-leaf philodendron, is a climbing evergreen vine in the arum family, Araceae [1]. It grows wild in rainforests from southern Mexico to Panama, where it climbs trees with long aerial roots [2]. Indoors it often reaches 1.8 to 2.4 m (6 to 8 ft) [1].

## Light

Monstera grows best in bright, indirect light, such as near an east- or west-facing window [3]. That light casts a clear shadow, but no sun falls on the leaves [5]. Spots more than about 1 m (a few feet) from a bright window are often too dim [5]. Strong midday sun can scorch the leaves [3,4]. In winter, when light is weaker, a brighter spot such as a south-facing window helps [2,4]. In low light, stems get leggy and new leaves have fewer holes or none [1–3].

## Watering

Water thoroughly, until water runs out of the drainage hole [5]. Then let the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of mix dry before you water again [1]. Test the mix with a finger rather than following a calendar [5]. If it still feels damp, check again in a day or two [5].

Empty the saucer after 30 to 45 minutes, and never leave the pot standing in water [3,5]. Water less often in winter, when every two weeks or so may be enough [3,4]. Use rainwater or filtered water at room temperature where you can, especially in hard-water areas [3].

## Temperature and Humidity

Keep your monstera at 18 to 25°C (65 to 77°F) all year, away from cold drafts and radiators [3]. Growth stops below 10°C (50°F), and frost kills the plant [2].

It likes medium to high humidity, preferably above 50% [1,2]. A humidifier is very effective [5]. Grouping plants also helps, as does a tray of wet pebbles with the pot sitting above the water [4,5]. Misting is a popular tip, but it raises humidity for only a few minutes and can help spread disease [5].

## Soil, Pots and Repotting

Use an open, well-draining mix [1,3]. The RHS suggests 1 part peat-free ericaceous (lime-free) mix to 2 parts orchid mix [3]. Penn State Extension suggests adding bark, perlite or charcoal to a bagged mix [1].

Always use a pot with a drainage hole [5]. Skip the gravel layer in the bottom: it keeps the mix above it wetter and promotes root rot [5]. For a decorative pot without a hole, set a draining pot inside it [5].

Repot every few years, when the plant gets top-heavy, growth slows or the mix dries out very fast [3,5]. Choose a heavy pot only a few centimeters (an inch or two) larger than the old one, since oversized pots stay wet too long [3]. Late winter or early spring is best [5].

## Feeding

Feed only while the plant is actively growing, which the RHS puts at April to September [3,5]. Use a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer every 2 to 4 weeks [1,5]. Mix a general all-purpose fertilizer at half or quarter strength [5]. Feed less or stop in winter, and wait 2 to 4 weeks after repotting [1,3,5].

## Support, Leaf Splits and Aerial Roots

Monstera is a climber, and its heavy stems need support so they don't break [2]. A moss pole is the usual choice; trellises, stakes and moss-covered boards also work [1,3]. Sink the support into the pot and tie the stem to it gently [1,2]. Keep the moss moist, so the aerial roots can root into it and take up water [2,3].

Young plants make small, uncut leaves. The holes and splits, called fenestrations, usually appear once a plant is a few years old [1,3]. In very low light, leaves have fewer holes or none [2,3]. A climbing plant often makes larger, more mature leaves with more holes [3].

Aerial roots are normal. They grow from the stem and anchor the plant as it climbs [2,3]. Guide them onto the moss pole or into the pot, since roots that reach the soil can root into it [2,3].

## Common Problems

- **No holes or splits in new leaves.** Likely cause: a young plant, low light or nothing to climb [2,3]. Fix: brighter indirect light, a moss pole and time [3].
- **Yellow leaves.** Likely cause: one lower leaf yellowing now and then is normal on a mature plant [4]. Many yellow leaves point to too much or too little water, an oversized pot, low light, cold or drafts [4]. Fix: check the roots and mix, then adjust watering, pot size or position [4]. See [yellow leaves](/guides/yellow-leaves).
- **Brown, dry leaf edges or tips.** Likely cause: dry air, or watering problems, more often too little water than too much [2,4]. Fix: check the roots, adjust watering and add a humidifier [4,5]. See [brown leaf tips](/guides/brown-leaf-tips).
- **Wilting in wet mix, with dark, soft roots.** Likely cause: root rot from overwatering or poor drainage [3,4]. Fix: water less, trim limp or brown roots, and repot into a smaller, draining pot if roots don't fill the old one [4,5]. See [root rot](/guides/root-rot).
- **Droplets on leaf edges in the morning.** Likely cause: guttation, often a sign of overwatering [2,4]. Fix: let the mix dry a little more between waterings [2].
- **Sticky leaves or small insects.** Likely cause: mealybugs, scale insects, aphids, spider mites or thrips [1–3]. Fix: wipe the leaves regularly, and for heavy infestations ask a local nursery or plant clinic [3].

## Propagation

Stem cuttings and air layering are the easiest methods [1]. Take cuttings from spring to late summer [3]. Cut the stem tip just below an aerial root [2]. Pot it up, or root it first in a glass of water in a bright spot [2,3]. Wear gloves, because the sap can irritate skin [1,3].

## Common Questions

### How Often Should I Water a Monstera?

There is no fixed schedule [5]. Check the mix at least once a week, and water thoroughly when the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) is dry [1,4]. In winter, water less often [3].

### Why Are My Monstera Leaves Not Splitting?

Young plants make uncut leaves, and holes usually appear after a few years [1,3]. Low light means fewer holes or none [2,3]. A moss pole helps, because a climbing plant often makes larger leaves with more holes [3].

### What Should I Do With Monstera Aerial Roots?

They are normal and help the plant climb [2,3]. Guide them onto a moist moss pole or into the pot, where they can root and take up water [2,3].

### Is Monstera Toxic to Cats?

Yes: the ASPCA lists it as toxic to cats and dogs because it contains insoluble calcium oxalates [6]. Chewing it can cause intense mouth irritation, drooling, vomiting and trouble swallowing [6]. Keep it out of reach, and call your vet if a pet chews it [3,6]. In the US, ASPCA Poison Control is at (888) 426-4435; a fee may apply [6].
