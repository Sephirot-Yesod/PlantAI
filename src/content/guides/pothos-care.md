# Pothos Care: How to Keep Your Vine Full and Healthy

Pothos keeps its color and full-size leaves in bright, indirect light of at least 1,600 lux (150 foot-candles) and does best at 18–30°C (65–86°F) [1][3]. Water it thoroughly once the top 4–5 cm (1.5–2 in) of soil is dry, and less often in fall and winter [4].

Golden pothos (*Epipremnum aureum*), also called devil's ivy, is an evergreen vine in the arum family (Araceae). It is native to the Society Islands of French Polynesia and climbs with aerial roots [2]. In the tropics, vines climb trees 15 m (50 ft) tall and grow lobed leaves up to about 90 cm (3 ft) long [3]. Indoors it usually keeps its heart-shaped juvenile leaves, which seldom pass 15 cm (6 in) [2][3].

## Light

Pothos prefers bright, indirect light [2]. An east- or west-facing window works, or a south-facing one behind a sheer curtain. Direct sun, especially in summer, can scorch the leaves [1].

Pothos survives long periods in low light [2] and tolerates levels as low as about 540 lux (50 foot-candles). At that level, new leaves stay small and variegated (patterned) types lose color. About 1,600 lux (150 foot-candles) or more keeps both color and leaf size [3]. Solid green 'Jade' is a good choice for dim rooms [2].

## Watering

Water thoroughly, then wait until the top 4–5 cm (1.5–2 in) of soil is dry [4]. Check with a finger, not a calendar [1]. Water less often in fall and winter [4]. In most homes, pothos does better a little on the dry side [3].

Soggy mix, from watering too often or from a pot left standing in water, can rot the roots [1]. Overwatering can also blacken leaf edges [2].

## Temperature and Humidity

For best results, keep pothos at 18–30°C (65–86°F), away from cold drafts and radiators [1]. UF/IFAS recommends 21–27°C (70–80°F) indoors. Several hours at 1–4°C (33–40°F), or an abrupt temperature change, can leave scattered brown patches in the center of leaves [3].

Unlike many tropical houseplants, pothos does not need high humidity, so normal room air is fine [1]. If winter heating makes the air very dry, a humidifier helps [2].

## Soil, Pots and Repotting

Use an open, slightly acidic mix that holds some moisture but drains well, in a pot with drainage holes. A peat-free houseplant mix works. For your own blend, the RHS suggests 3 parts acidic (ericaceous) peat-free mix, 1 part grit and 1 part medium orchid bark [1].

Repot in spring once roots crowd the pot or grow through the drainage holes, roughly every 2 to 3 years. Go up only one pot size; a much larger pot stays wet and risks root rot [1]. Wait about 4 weeks before repotting or feeding a newly bought plant [3].

## Feeding

Feed once a month from April to October with a general houseplant fertilizer, at the label rate [1]. NC State Extension suggests every other month; both sources skip winter [1][2]. Built-up fertilizer salts can damage leaves and roots, and running plenty of plain water through the pot may help [3].

## Pruning and Training

Older leaves yellow and drop over time, leaving most leaves near the stem tips [2]. To keep the plant bushy, cut long stems back to just above a leaf. New shoots grow from the leaf joints, and the cut pieces make good cuttings [1].

Let stems trail, or train them up a moss- or coir-covered pole kept damp so the aerial roots grip it [1]. With support and enough light, pothos starts producing larger, mature leaves [2]. Wear gloves when pruning, because the plant can irritate skin [1][2].

## Common Problems

- **Older leaves turn yellow.** Likely cause: soil kept too wet or too dry [3]; an occasional old leaf yellowing is normal aging [2]. Fix: if the soil is wet, let the top 4–5 cm (1.5–2 in) dry before watering; if bone dry, water thoroughly [4]. See [yellow leaves](/guides/yellow-leaves).
- **Black leaf edges and soil that stays wet.** Likely cause: overwatering and [root rot](/guides/root-rot) [2]. Fix: water less, and root a few healthy stem pieces as backups [1].
- **Small, plain new leaves.** Likely cause: too little light; in commercial growing, low fertilizer or magnesium can do the same. Fix: give it 1,600 lux (150 fc) or more of indirect light [3].
- **Dry, brown patches on the pale parts of leaves.** Likely cause: direct sun or heat. Fix: move it out of direct sun and away from heat; damaged leaves will not recover [3].
- **Scattered brown patches in leaf centers after a cold spell.** Likely cause: chilling at 1–4°C (33–40°F) or an abrupt temperature drop [3]. Fix: keep it at 18–30°C (65–86°F), away from drafts [1].
- **Crisp, brown leaf tips.** Possible causes: fertilizer salt buildup [3] or dry heat from a radiator [1]. Fix: flush the pot with plain water and move the plant away from heat. See [brown leaf tips](/guides/brown-leaf-tips).
- **White, cottony clumps or shell-like bumps on stems and leaves.** Likely cause: mealybugs or scale insects [2]. Fix: check plants often, and ask a local nursery or plant clinic about treatment if they spread.

## Propagation

Pothos roots easily from stem cuttings, even in a glass of water [1][2].

1. Cut a piece of healthy stem just below a leaf joint, or node [1]. Commercial growers root pieces as short as 2.5–4 cm (1–1.5 in), with one node and one leaf [3].
2. Stand it in a glass of water in a bright spot, with the node under water [1].
3. Roots often show within 2 to 4 weeks [2][3].
4. Once roots appear, pot the cutting up in potting mix [1].

To layer a stem instead, pin it onto a pot of free-draining mix, and roots should form at the leaf joint [1].

## Common Questions

### Can Pothos Live in Low Light?

Yes. It survives long periods in low light and tolerates about 540 lux (50 foot-candles) [2][3]. New leaves will be smaller and variegated types fade, so solid green 'Jade' suits dim spots better [2][3].

### Why Is My Pothos Losing Its Variegation?

Too little light is the usual cause [1][2][3]. Variegated pothos needs about 1,600 lux (150 foot-candles) or more to keep its color [3]. Move it nearer a bright window, out of direct sun, and watch the new leaves [1][3].

### Is Pothos Toxic to Cats and Dogs?

Yes. The ASPCA lists it as toxic to cats and dogs because it contains insoluble calcium oxalates [5]. Signs include burning and irritation of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting and trouble swallowing [5]. If your pet eats some, call your vet; in the US, ASPCA Poison Control is (888) 426-4435, and a fee may apply [5].

### Does Pothos Clean Indoor Air?

Not in a meaningful way. The idea grew from a 1989 NASA study of plants in small, sealed chambers [6]. Homes constantly exchange air and gain new pollutants, so even about 11 plants per square meter (1 per square foot) cannot keep up [6].
