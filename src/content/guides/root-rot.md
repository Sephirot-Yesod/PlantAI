# Root Rot in Houseplants: Signs, Rescue and Prevention

A plant with root rot wilts or yellows while its soil is still wet, and its roots turn brown or black and soft. Don't add more water: first slide the plant out of its pot and check the roots.

## Quick Diagnosis

| What you see | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wilting while the soil is still wet | Rotting roots can't take up water [1][5] | Don't water. Check the roots. |
| Wilting, and the pot feels light | The plant is probably just dry [5] | Water well. Very dry mix may need a soak [5]. |
| Older, lower leaves yellow and drop; the plant looks stunted | Possibly root rot, which can look like a nutrient shortage [1][3]. Low light, cold and overwatering cause this too [4]. | Check the mix and roots. See [yellow leaves](/guides/yellow-leaves). |
| Brown or black, soft roots that may smell bad | Root rot [1][2][5] | Trim the rot and repot, or start over. |
| Root skin slides off when pulled, leaving a thin thread | That root has rotted [3] | Cut it back to firm, white tissue. |
| Brown or black ring around the stem at soil level | Rot has reached the stem [2] | Root cuttings from healthy shoots [5]. |
| Firm, white roots, but the mix stays soggy | Too much water or poor drainage, but no rot yet [2][4] | Let the mix dry more between waterings. Check that the pot drains. |
| Brown leaf tips or edges, healthy roots | Probably not root rot; salt buildup is one cause [5] | See [brown leaf tips](/guides/brown-leaf-tips). |

## What Causes Root Rot

Roots need air as well as water. In waterlogged potting mix, water fills the air spaces and roots can drown [6]. Overwatering also favors root diseases [2].

The main culprits are the water molds Pythium and Phytophthora and the fungi Rhizoctonia and Fusarium [1][4]. Water molds spread by swimming spores and are not true fungi [4]. Most of these organisms thrive in wet mix, and some survive a long time in soil or plant debris [1]. They can come from garden soil, reused mix, drainage water or infected new plants [1][4]. Fungus gnats can spread them too [4].

Common reasons a mix stays too wet:

- Watering too often [2].
- No drainage holes, or a pot left standing in water [2][5].
- A pot much larger than the root system [5][7].
- Old, compacted mix, or roots so crowded they block drainage [4][7].
- Cold: wet mix plus cold temperatures often leads to root rot [5].

Salt buildup from fertilizer is sometimes linked to root rot as well [4][5]. You can't tell by eye whether roots died from waterlogging or infection; it often takes a lab test [3][6]. By the time leaves show symptoms, root decay is often well advanced [6].

## How to Check the Roots

Check the roots whenever a plant wilts, before you water again [4][5].

- **Unpot it.** Hold the main stems and lift while holding the pot down. If it sticks, lay the pot on its side or tip it upside down, cradling the root ball [7].
- **Feel and smell.** Wet, foul-smelling mix at the bottom of the pot points to overwatering and rot [5].
- **Rinse.** Wash some mix off so you can see the roots [3].
- **Compare.** Healthy roots are white, at least under the outer skin, and firm rather than limp [3][4]. Rotted roots are brownish to black and soft, often starting at the tips [2][3][4].
- **Pull gently.** If the outer layer slides off and leaves a thin, stringy core, the root has rotted [3].
- **Check the stem base.** A brown or black ring at soil level means rot has reached the stem [2].

Some plants have naturally brown roots, so judge texture and the pull test, not just color [3]. Only a lab can identify the organism [3][6], so for a valuable plant or several sick plants, ask a plant clinic.

## How to Save a Plant With Root Rot

A rescue works best when only part of the root system has rotted [2].

1. **Unpot the plant** as described above, without watering it first.
2. **Remove the old mix.** Shake and rinse it off the roots [3]. Throw it away, along with any drainage water, since both can carry root rot organisms [1].
3. **Trim the rot.** With clean scissors or pruners, cut off every soft, brown or black root, back to firm, white tissue [2][4][7].
4. **Judge what is left.** If only a few roots were rotted, carry on. If most were dark, soft and dead, go to When to Start Over [2].
5. **Disinfect tools and work surfaces** with a 10% bleach or detergent solution, or alcohol [1].
6. **Choose a clean pot with drainage holes.** Use a new pot, or a clay pot scrubbed and rinsed in 1 part household bleach to 9 parts water [1][2]. University of Wisconsin–Madison specialists advise throwing out plastic pots that held a plant with root rot [1]. Pick a size that fits the remaining roots, since too large a pot invites rot [5].
7. **Repot in fresh mix.** Use bagged potting mix, never garden soil, and skip any gravel layer [1]. Set the root ball's top just below the rim, then fill and firm gently [7].
8. **Water once, then wait.** Water until a little drains from the holes [7], then empty the saucer [2]. After that, give enough water to prevent drought stress, but don't overwater [1].
9. **Give it time.** Keep the plant warm and away from cold windows [5]. Skip fertilizer until it grows again, since excess fertilizer can also harm roots [3]. Dead roots don't recover; the plant must grow new ones [3].

## When to Start Over

UW–Madison specialists say throwing out a plant with root rot is often the best and most cost-effective choice [1]. Start over when most roots are dark, soft and dead [2], or when rot rings the stem at soil level [2].

If some shoots are still firm and free of rot, take stem cuttings from them and root them [5]. Use only stems with no dark or soft spots. Then discard the old plant, its mix and, if it is plastic, its pot [1]. Clean any tools and surfaces it touched [1].

## How to Prevent Root Rot

- **Check before you water.** Lift small pots: a light pot needs water [5]. A plant that wilts in wet mix needs a root check, not more water [1][5].
- **Use pots with drainage holes.** Repot plants growing in pots without them [5].
- **Never leave pots standing in water.** Empty saucers and decorative outer pots after watering [1][2].
- **Skip the gravel layer.** Rocks or gravel in the bottom of a pot do not improve drainage. UW–Madison says they can actually inhibit it [1].
- **Size up gradually.** A new pot should be no more than a third larger than the old one. For pots under 25 cm (10 in) across, go about 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider and deeper [7].
- **Refresh old mix.** Mix compacts as it ages, so repot every few years [4][7].
- **Use fresh potting mix, never garden soil,** which often contains root rot organisms [1].
- **Keep plants away from cold drafts,** especially when the mix is wet [5].
- **Go easy on fertilizer,** and now and then run plenty of water through the pot to wash out salts [3][4][5].
- **Inspect new plants.** Before you buy, slide the plant out of its pot and pass on any with discolored roots or stems [1][4].

## Common Questions

### What Does Root Rot Look Like?

Rotted roots are brown to black, soft or limp, and may smell bad [1][2][3]. Their outer layer often slides off when pulled, leaving a thin thread [3]. Above ground, look for wilting in wet mix, yellowing leaves and slow growth [1].

### Can a Plant Recover From Root Rot?

Yes, if only a few roots are affected and you trim them and repot in fresh mix [2]. Dead roots don't recover, so the plant must grow new ones [3]. If most roots are dark, soft and dead, Clemson Extension advises discarding the plant, though healthy shoots may still root as cuttings [2][5].

### Is It Overwatering or Root Rot?

Often both: overwatering cuts the oxygen roots need and favors root diseases [2]. Roots killed by waterlogging can look just like roots killed by Phytophthora, and telling them apart often takes a lab test [6]. Either way, the rescue starts the same: check the roots, remove the dead ones and water less.

### Do Hydrogen Peroxide or Fungicides Help?

UW–Madison does not recommend fungicides for houseplant root rot, because few products are sold to home gardeners and those are expensive [1]. In the UK, the Royal Horticultural Society says no chemical treatments are available to gardeners for Phytophthora root rot [6]. Fungicides work only against certain groups of organisms and cannot revive dead roots [3]. None of the sources behind this guide recommend hydrogen peroxide.
