# Snake Plant Care: Water Less and Keep It Thriving for Years

Water a snake plant only when its potting mix feels dry a finger's depth down, and in winter only every one to two months. It prefers a bright spot with 2 to 6 hours of direct sun and 15–24°C (60–75°F), but tolerates low light.

Snake plant, also called mother-in-law's tongue, is an evergreen succulent with stiff, sword-shaped leaves that rise from a thick underground stem. Botanists now call it *Dracaena trifasciata*, after genetic studies linked sansevierias to dracaenas. Its old name, *Sansevieria trifasciata*, is still used by some references, including the ASPCA. It grows wild in tropical West and Central Africa. Like most sansevierias, it comes from hot, dry habitats and stores water in its fleshy leaves.

## Light

Snake plant prefers a warm, bright spot. NC State Extension suggests a place with 2 to 6 hours of direct sun a day. In summer, shade it from hot midday and afternoon sun, which can scorch the leaves.

It also tolerates very low light, according to NC State. In very dim spots, though, tall kinds may flop. For low light, the Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) suggests all-green kinds such as 'Hahnii' or 'Moonshine'. Give the pot a quarter turn at each watering so the plant does not lean toward the light.

## Watering

Overwatering is the main cause of trouble, because the roots rot in wet mix. Water only when the potting mix has dried out; push a finger in to check rather than watering on a schedule. Pour water onto the mix, not into the center of the leaf rosette, and empty the saucer afterward.

In winter, growth slows and the plant draws on water stored in its leaves. NC State suggests watering only every one to two months. A plant in a very warm spot may need a little more, the RHS notes.

## Temperature and Humidity

The RHS gives 15–24°C (60–75°F) as the ideal range. NC State says it tolerates cool temperatures around 10°C (50°F), so treat that as the lower limit. Keep it away from cold drafts and radiators. It can spend summer outdoors in a lightly shaded spot if moved out gradually, but bring it in well before any frost.

Low humidity suits it, so dry indoor air is fine. The RHS advises never misting it and keeping it out of steamy bathrooms.

## Soil, Pots and Repotting

Use a free-draining cactus potting mix, or the RHS mix of two parts loam-based potting mix to one part horticultural grit. Choose a pot with plenty of drainage holes. A clay pot wider than it is tall keeps a tall plant from toppling, the Missouri Botanical Garden notes.

Snake plants like a snug pot, and new plants are usually happy in theirs for the first few years. Repot only when roots and leaves fill the pot or make it buckle. Go only slightly larger, because extra mix stays damp and invites root rot.

## Feeding

Feed only in the growing season. To boost growth, the RHS suggests a liquid houseplant fertilizer or liquid cactus fertilizer once a month from April to September. Skip feeding from October to March, when growth slows.

## Does It Clean the Air?

Not in any way you would notice at home. The claim often cites a 1989 NASA report. It measured how much benzene and formaldehyde houseplants, including snake plant, removed from sealed test chambers over 24 hours.

Homes are not sealed boxes, though. A 2020 review by Cummings and Waring analyzed 196 results from 12 chamber studies. Matching the cleaning that outdoor air already provides in a typical building would take 10 to 1,000 potted plants per square meter of floor. That is about 1 to 93 plants per square foot. For cleaner air, ventilation matters far more.

## Common Problems

- **Soft, mushy leaves, or a plant that wobbles in its pot.** Likely cause: root rot from too much water or a waterlogged saucer. Fix: Stop watering and unpot the plant. Keep only firm sections with healthy roots and repot them in fresh, dry cactus mix. See [root rot](/guides/root-rot).
- **Leaves flopping or falling over.** Likely cause: too little light for a tall kind, or a narrow pot. If the whole plant leans or leaves collapse, suspect root rot or vine weevil grubs eating the roots. Fix: Give it more light, support leaves with a hoop or canes, use a wide clay pot and check the roots.
- **Brown, scorched patches.** Likely cause: hot midday or afternoon summer sun. Fix: Move it out of strong sun and cut badly damaged leaves off at the base. For brown tips, see [brown leaf tips](/guides/brown-leaf-tips).
- **Yellow leaves.** Likely cause: on kinds such as 'Laurentii', yellow edges are normal. Fix: Treat a leaf that turns yellow and soft like root rot, above. For other causes, see [yellow leaves](/guides/yellow-leaves).
- **Small insects between the leaf bases.** Likely cause: mealybugs; in hot, dry rooms, spider mites are also possible. Fix: Wipe off what you can, isolate the plant and ask a local nursery or plant clinic if pests return.

## Propagation

Propagate in spring, from March to May, as the plant comes back into growth. Wear gloves, because the sap can irritate skin.

- **Division.** Unpot the plant and pull off well-rooted offsets, or split a large clump into pieces with leaves and roots. Pot them up, staking tall pieces with a cane until they root in.
- **Leaf cuttings.** Slice a mature leaf across into 5 cm (2 in) sections, noting which end was lower. Let the cuts dry for a day or two, then push the lower half of each piece into damp cuttings mix. Keep them at 18°C (65°F) with bottom heat, or in a clear bag in a warm room.

Cuttings from variegated kinds, such as yellow-edged 'Laurentii', tend to grow into plain green plants, so divide those instead.

## Common Questions

### How Often Should I Water a Snake Plant?

Water only when the potting mix has dried out; check by pushing a finger into it. In winter, NC State suggests watering only every one to two months.

### Can Snake Plants Live in Low Light?

Yes. It tolerates very low light, though it prefers a bright spot and tall kinds may flop in very dim rooms.

### How Do I Care for a Snake Plant in Winter?

Water only every one to two months and stop feeding until April. Keep it at 10°C (50°F) or warmer, away from cold drafts and radiators.

### Why Are My Snake Plant's Leaves Falling Over?

Tall kinds can flop in very low light, so move the plant somewhere brighter and support the leaves. If the whole plant leans or the leaf bases are soft, check the roots for rot.

### Is Snake Plant Toxic to Cats?

Yes. The ASPCA lists snake plant as toxic to cats and dogs; its saponins can cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhea. NC State rates the severity as low, but keep it out of reach. If your pet eats some, call your vet or, in the US, ASPCA Poison Control at (888) 426-4435.
