# Brown Leaf Tips on Houseplants: Causes and Fixes

Brown, crispy leaf tips and edges usually come from dry air, uneven watering or a build-up of salts from fertilizer and hard water [2][5]. Check the soil first: feel 5 cm (2 in) down for dry or soggy mix, and look for a white crust on top [3][5].

## Quick Diagnosis

| What you see | Likely cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tips and edges brown, worse in winter | Low humidity | Run a humidifier; move plants away from heaters |
| Browning after the mix dried out | Dry soil or uneven watering | Water thoroughly; check the mix first next time |
| White crust on the soil or pot rim | Salt build-up | Scrape off crust; flush with twice the pot's volume of water |
| Wet soil; brown, soft or smelly roots | Overwatering and root rot | Let the mix dry; trim rotten roots and repot |
| Roots fill or circle the pot | Root-bound plant | Move to a larger pot; loosen the roots |
| Slow tip burn on spider plant, dracaena or calathea | Fluoride or other chemicals in tap water | Switch to rainwater or filtered water |
| Sunburn-like patches after a move into sun | Too much direct sun or heat | Move out of direct sun; adjust light gradually |
| Browned or blackened growth after cold or drafts | Cold damage | Keep plants 15 cm (6 in) from cold glass and outside doors |

## Low Humidity

Iowa State Extension calls low humidity the most common reason for brown leaf tips, and indoor air is often driest in winter [2]. Many houseplants grow well at 40 to 60 percent relative humidity [1]. NC State Extension puts the average in heated or air-conditioned homes at 10 to 15 percent [1].

**How to tell:** Tips die and edges brown, sometimes with yellowing or curling [1]. Cacti, succulents and bromeliads cope with dry air, so check other causes for them [1].

**What to do:** A humidifier near the plants works best; keep humidity below about 60 percent to avoid mold [1]. Grouping plants and pebble trays also help; keep the water below the pebble tops so the pot never sits in it [1]. Move plants away from radiators and heat vents, which dry the air [2][6]. Skip the spray bottle: misting raises humidity for only a short time [1].

## Dry Soil and Uneven Watering

Letting the mix dry out too long between waterings browns tips and edges [2][5].

**How to tell:** The mix is dry 5 cm (2 in) down, and the pot feels light [3]. Water may run down the inside of the pot without soaking in [2].

**What to do:** Water by need, not on a schedule [3]. Water until it drains from the bottom so the whole root ball is wet, then empty the saucer [2][3]. Soak a bone-dry root ball in water [1]. Cacti and succulents need far less water [3]. ## Salt Build-Up in the Soil

Fertilizer salts and hard-water minerals stay behind as water evaporates from the mix [3][5]. Avoid softened water, which carries more dissolved minerals than other water [3].

**How to tell:** A white to gray crust forms on the soil, the pot rim or the outside of clay pots [3][5]. Tips brown, lower leaves may drop and growth slows [3].

**What to do:** Leach the mix every four to six months [3]:

1. Scrape off any salt crust.
2. Pour at least twice the pot's volume of water through, and let it drain completely. A 2-liter (2-quart) pot needs at least 4 liters (about 1 gallon).
3. Leach before you fertilize, not after.
4. Repot if salts seem high or the pot drains poorly.

Don't let the pot sit in the drained water, or salts can soak back in [1]. Fertilize less, and use rainwater where you can [2][5].

## Root Damage: Overwatering, Rot and Crowded Roots

Roots that rot or run out of room can't supply enough water, so leaves brown as if dry [2][4].

**How to tell:** Slide the plant out of its pot. Rotting roots are brown and soft and may smell; an underwatered plant's roots are white [1]. Wet soil starves roots of oxygen; watering too often, no drainage hole or a full saucer are common reasons [2][5]. In a root-bound plant, roots fill or circle the pot, and water runs straight through [4].

**What to do:** For rot, let the mix dry, trim off brown roots and repot in fresh mix in a pot with drainage holes [1][2]. Badly rotted plants may not recover; see our [root rot guide](/guides/root-rot) [1]. Overwatering often shows as [yellow leaves](/guides/yellow-leaves) too [5]. Move a root-bound plant to a larger pot, loosening the root mass by hand or cutting some away [4].

## Fluoride and Water Quality

Iowa State Extension says chlorine, fluoride and other water chemicals can brown tips over time on sensitive plants [2]. It names spider plant, ti plant, dracaena, prayer plant and calathea [2]. The RHS links fluoride in tap water to brown spider plant tips [6].

**How to tell:** Suspect your water when one of these plants keeps browning slowly after humidity and watering are fixed [2].

**What to do:** Use rainwater, filtered water or distilled water for sensitive plants [3][6]. NC State notes that perlite contains some fluoride, so leave it out of their potting mix [1]. Letting tap water stand for a day lets chlorine escape [3]. For fluoride, Iowa State and the RHS advise changing the water instead [2][6].

## Too Much Sun or Heat

Most houseplants come from shady understory habitats and can scorch in strong light [2]. Sun through a window can overheat leaves even when the room feels comfortable [1]. Hot, dry air from radiators, vents and appliances also browns tips and edges [1][5].

**How to tell:** Scorch looks like sunburn [2]. It often follows a quick move to brighter light, such as outdoors [2].

**What to do:** Move the plant out of direct sun [2]. Shift plants to brighter spots gradually, over several weeks [2]. Keep them away from heaters and warm appliances, and don't aim fans at them [1]. Scorch damage is permanent; trim it if you like [2].

## Cold Damage

NC State Extension says many tropical houseplants suffer chilling injury below about 10 to 13 °C (50 to 55 °F) [1]. Most grow best near 21 °C (70 °F) by day and 16 °C (60 °F) at night [1].

**How to tell:** Browned, blackened or wilted growth after a cold trip, a cold window or a draft points to cold [1][2]. Damage can take weeks to appear [1].

**What to do:** Move plants about 15 cm (6 in) back from cold windows and away from outside doors [1][2]. Don't trap them behind curtains at night [2]. In cold weather, wrap plants for even short trips and carry them in the heated car [1][2]. A minimum/maximum thermometer shows how cold a windowsill gets [1].

## Should You Trim Brown Tips?

Yes. Trimming is fine, but it is cosmetic: new browning will appear unless you fix the cause [2][6].

- If less than a third of a leaf is brown, snip off the dead tip with scissors but leave a thin brown edge [1][2]. NC State says this keeps the leaf from dying back further [1].
- Remove mostly brown leaves at the base [1][6].
- Calatheas need no other pruning, but you can snip off faded brown leaves [7].

## How to Prevent Brown Tips

- Check the mix 5 cm (2 in) down, water by need, and empty saucers [3].
- In winter, run a humidifier; 40 to 60 percent suits many houseplants [1][2].
- Keep plants away from heaters, outside doors and cold glass [1][2].
- Don't over-fertilize, and leach the mix every four to six months [3][5].
- Give fluoride-sensitive plants rainwater or filtered water [2][6].
- Repot when roots fill the pot [4].
- Check for pests: insect feeding also browns tips, and fine webbing can mean spider mites [5][7].

## Common Questions

### Why Does My Calathea Have Crispy Edges?

The RHS says brown tips and edges on calatheas usually come from low humidity [7]. Keep them at 16 to 21 °C (61 to 70 °F), out of direct sun and away from drafts and radiators [7]. Keep the mix evenly moist in spring and summer [7]. Iowa State also lists calathea as sensitive to tap-water chemicals, so try rainwater [2].

### Why Does My Spider Plant Get Brown Tips?

The RHS names too much or too little water, fluoride in tap water, bright sun and very dry air [6]. It suggests rainwater or filtered water and mix that stays lightly moist [6]. Leave perlite out of the potting mix, since it contains some fluoride [1]. A badly browned plant can be cut back to 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 in) to regrow [6].

### Does Misting Stop Brown Tips?

Not reliably. NC State Extension says misting raises humidity for only a short time and is a major cause of bacterial leaf diseases [1]. A humidifier near your plants works better [1].
